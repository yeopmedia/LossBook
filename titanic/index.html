Where accounts differ

Where the records disagree, we follow the document made closest to the event, and say so here.

  • Two clocks that night. The Californian kept her own ship's time, and the American inquiry gave many messages in New York time. The narration gives the Californian's times "by her own clock" (MER Part V; BWC day 8, Q8111–8265 (Groves)) and gives the Titanic's times by her clock.
  • What the Titanic's operator answered. The Californian's operator, Cyril Evans, told the American inquiry he was told to shut up because the Titanic was busy working Cape Race (USH pp.701–717); the American report gives the same words (SR806 pp.6–7). At the British inquiry he said the answer was "Keep out" (BWC day 8, Q8979–9028 (Evans)), and the British report gives that (MER Part V). The narration gives both, each with its inquiry. Evans put his last message at about 11 p.m. by his ship's clock, 9:05 in New York (USH pp.701–717; BWC day 8, Q8979–9028 (Evans)).
  • Haze ahead. The lookout Reginald Lee spoke of a haze right ahead (BWC day 4, Q2398–2418, 2600–2613, 2658–2667, 2722–2731); his partner Frederick Fleet said it did not affect them (BWC day 15, Q17245–17273, 17390–17400, 17475); the fourth officer described an oily calm (USH pt. 3, pp.252–253, 256–257). The British report found a clear night with no moon (MER Part II 'The weather conditions'). The narration gives the opposing words side by side.
  • Lifeboat No. 6 and the people in the water. Fleet said the women in the boat wanted to go back and the quartermaster would not (USH pt. 4, pp.326–329, 358–359, 366); Major Peuchen's account of what the quartermaster said was put to him at the American inquiry (USH pt. 5, pp.452–455; USH pp.336–338). Robert Hichens denied the words and said they were too far away and had no compass (USH pt. 5, pp.452–455; BWC day 3, Q1327–1377). The narration gives his answer and does not decide between them.
  • What was reported at 2 a.m. on the Californian. The second officer, Stone, said the ship was disappearing (BWC day 7, Q7962–7982, 8048–8050 (Stone)); the apprentice, Gibson, said he reported that she had disappeared in the south-west (BWC day 7, Q7552–7617 (Gibson)); Captain Lord said he did not hear the word disappeared (BWC day 7, Q6862, 7274–7317, 7373–7376 (Lord)). The narration quotes Gibson's report.
  • Lights after the rockets. Stone saw a white light far to the south shortly after 3:20 (BWC day 7, Q8008–8021 (Stone)); Gibson put flashes on the horizon near dawn (BWC day 7, Q7552–7617 (Gibson)); the chief officer gave Stone's sighting as about 3:40 (BWC day 8, Q8576–8652, 8757–8769, 8778–8783, 8856–8868, 8908–8918 (Stewart)). Whether these were the Carpathia's rockets is not settled in the testimony, so the narration gives Stone's own account only.
  • When the rescue ended. The British report has the boats spread over 4 or 5 miles and all picked up by 8 a.m. (MER Part IV 'The rescue by the steamship Carpathia'); the American report has the last boat at about 8:30 (SR806 p.13). The narration follows the British report.
  • When the Californian heard. Her operator, by his own account, was woken at about 5:40 by her clock (BWC day 8, Q9051–9107, 9148–9150, 9162–9169 (Evans)); Captain Lord told the American inquiry he first heard between 5 and 5:30 (USI day 8 (Lord)); the Carpathia's captain saw the Californian at about 8 a.m. (USI day 1 (Rostron)), while the Californian's log has her alongside at 8:30 (BWC day 8, Q8658–8756, 8784–8790, 8812–8855, 8902–8905 (Stewart)). The narration gives the operator's time, by his own account.
  • How many were saved. The British inquiry counted 2,201 aboard and 711 saved (MER Part IV 'Numbers saved'); the American inquiry counted 2,223 aboard, 1,517 lost and 706 saved (SR806 pp.5–6). The Carpathia took on 712, one of whom died soon after (MER Part IV 'The rescue by the steamship Carpathia'). The narration gives the British count and says the American count differs.
  • Children saved. Some later accounts say five of six first-class children were saved. The British report's table, checked against the original British edition, has every first- and second-class child saved and 27 of 79 third-class children (MER Part IV 'Numbers saved'; CD6352 p.42). The narration follows the table.
  • Bodies recovered. The Nova Scotia Archives give about 337 in all (NSA); counting the archive's own ship-by-ship lists gives a slightly different total, because some ships have no list there. The narration gives the archive's figure and names the archive.
  • The crowd at the pier. Later accounts often give about 40,000; the New-York Tribune of April 19, 1912 gives a police estimate of 100,000 to 150,000 (N7). The narration gives the police estimate and says so.
  • The depth of the wreck. The first report, just after the discovery, gave about 13,000 feet (OC1 p.33); after the 1986 dives the figure was about 12,500 feet (OC2 pp.2–15), which NOAA gives as 3,800 metres (NOAA). The narration gives 3,800 metres.
  • Where she sank. The British court accepted the position from the distress calls (MER Part VII question 18 (table)); the wreck lies some 13 miles from it (MAIB pp.3, 5–6; MAIB pp.7, 9). The narration says miles away, as the 1992 reappraisal does.
